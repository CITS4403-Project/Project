\section{Discussion and conclusions}
\label{sec:discussion}

The tree topology explains RQ1's vulnerability: removing a station can cut the
only rail path between corridors. Extra capacity cannot restore a deleted edge.
RQ2 instead concerns whether surviving facilities acquire further overloads.
Static redistribution keeps forwarding a proxy quantity locally; dynamic
betweenness removes contributions from unreachable pairs. Their sharp contrast
reveals the importance of how disrupted journeys are represented. Interpreting
the dynamic result as uninterrupted operation would confuse disappearing load
with preserved service. Likewise, the static all-station cascade is conditional
on the forwarding rule, not evidence that a single real closure would destroy
Perth's railway.

RQ3 shows why recovery needs both service and facility metrics. Backup paths
can reconnect terminals while increasing load at particular rail facilities;
the Fremantle case supplies a traced overload mechanism. Enough substitute
connectivity can maintain travel despite that extra closure, whereas a limited
selection can leave the network worse after secondary failures. Policies should
therefore be compared after the cascade stabilises, with a common intact OD
denominator and the reachable population reported beside travel times. Our
two-link budget counts selected connections, not physical buses or fleet cost.

Uncertainty remains broader than the Monte Carlo intervals in RQ4. We study
one service date and window, mapped undirected links, approximate rail speeds,
proxy capacities, symmetric effective bus times and immediate unlimited bus
standby. The model omits measured boarding/alighting, vehicle occupancy,
directional timetables, congestion, dispatch delay and finite substitute
capacity. Terminal-pair reachability is not proof of a feasible scheduled
journey. The finite 86-station support also limits avalanche-tail inference;
the rejected bounded hypothesis cannot establish the absence of scaling in a
larger or different transport system.

The most useful next tests would introduce observed OD demand and vehicle
capacity, directional time-dependent routing, delayed finite bus deployment,
and several service-day snapshots. Enumerating all 86 deterministic station
triggers would remove trigger-sampling error, while confidence intervals across
dates would address a different source of uncertainty. The present conclusions
are narrower: targeted corridor removal is structurally damaging; overload
containment depends on the update rule and tolerance grid; and substitute
connectivity can improve service while aggravating rail overload. These are
reproducible mechanisms to test against operational evidence, rather than a
recommendation to deploy a particular bus policy.
